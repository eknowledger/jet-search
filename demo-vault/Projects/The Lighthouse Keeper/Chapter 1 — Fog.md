# Chapter 1 — Fog

*Part One — Fog. Draft 4.*

---

The fog came in a little after four, the way it did at the end of summer, not rolling but simply arriving, as if the sea had decided to stand up and walk inland. Elena saw it first from the gallery. One moment the horizon was a hard grey line with a fishing boat pinned to it; the next there was no line and no boat, only a whiteness that took the island piece by piece — the far headland, the goat path, the roof of the oil store, the wall her grandfather had built — until it reached the tower and she was standing inside it.

She went down the iron stairs counting, as she always did. Ninety-six steps. Her mother had taught her to count them when she was small, one hand on the rail, and she had never been able to stop. At sixty she could smell the paraffin. At eighty she could hear the clock that turned the lens, its slow heavy tick, the weight going down the hollow of the tower on its chain like a bucket into a well.

Her father was at the table in the service room with the log book open and his pen not moving. He had his back to the window. He had not seen.

"Pai. The fog."

He did not turn round. She said it again, louder, and then she crossed the room and put her hand on his shoulder, and he startled the way a horse startles, the whole body at once, and looked up at her with an expression she had begun to hate — the brief, naked fear, and then the face closing over it like water.

"The fog," she said, facing him, slowly, so that he could see her mouth.

"I know," said Tomás Varga. "I can smell it."

Fog had a smell on Flores: wet stone, cows, the kelp below the cliffs. But he had not smelt it. He had been sitting there writing nothing, and the fog had come in behind him, and if she had been in the village, or asleep, or gone — she thought the word, *gone*, and put it away — he would have gone on sitting there until the light failed in the window and told him.

He stood, and they went to the horn together.

The fog signal at Ponta Negra was a compressed-air horn installed in 1931, and it lived in its own squat house at the edge of the cliff, with the engine and the tanks and a smell of oil that never left your clothes. Starting it was a thing for two people. Her father primed the engine and she turned the flywheel, without speaking, his hand giving her the rhythm. The engine caught on the third turn. Then he pulled the lever and the horn spoke.

It was not a sound so much as a weather of its own. Two blasts, every thirty seconds. She had heard it all her life — she had been born, Dona Graça said, on a night of fog with the horn going, and had come out of her mother red and furious, as if she wanted to argue with it — and still it went into the bones of her face and made her teeth ache.

Her father stood with one hand flat against the wall of the horn house.

She had noticed it in the spring and had not understood. He would stand like that, palm against the stone, head a little bowed, like a man listening at a door. Now she understood. He was not listening. He was feeling the horn through the wall, the shudder of it, counting the blasts with his hand because he could no longer count them with his ears.

"It's good," he said. "Two and thirty. Good."

"It's good," she said.

---

They had their supper standing up, bread and cheese and the last of the cold fish, because in fog there was no sitting down. Her father went up to light the lamp at the hour written in the book, though the hour meant nothing now; the sun was somewhere behind the white, going down on its own. She heard him on the stairs, the slow climb, the stop at the landing where his knee bothered him. Then the hiss of the burner as he pumped the paraffin, the soft cough of the mantle catching, and the beam came on above her, invisible from inside the fog, and yet she felt it, a turning she could not see, the way you feel someone look at you across a room.

She took the bucket and went out to the oil store, though there was nothing she needed from it.

Outside, the world had shrunk to the length of her arm. The horn blew, and blew. Between the blasts there was a silence so thick she could hear the sea breathing far below, and the bell of a cow somewhere up on the pasture, and her own blood. She let herself think about Lisbon.

She had no pictures of it, or very few: a postcard her aunt had sent of the Praça do Comércio with its yellow arches and a tram like a toy; a photograph in the newspaper of the new bridge they were going to build across the Tagus, which did not yet exist. She thought about it as a sound. A city must be loud, she thought. A city must be so full of noise that no one could ever be alone with a horn and a fog and a father who could hear neither. She imagined trams and church bells and a thousand radios, and herself walking through it all unnoticed, and the thought was so sweet and so shameful that she pressed her forehead against the cold wall of the oil store until the next blast of the horn.

Nineteen. Tia Lurdes had gone at seventeen. Her mother had not gone at all.

---

At ten she relieved him. That was not in the book; in the book it said *Tomás Varga, keeper*, in his square careful hand. But since the spring it was Elena who took the first half of the night, until three, and he who slept, or lay awake — she did not know which — in the room below.

On a clear night the lamp room gave you the whole sea. Tonight the beam went nowhere. It struck the fog a few metres from the glass and lit it from inside, so that the tower seemed to stand in the middle of a turning white cloud, a lantern inside a lantern. The great lens went round on its bath of mercury, silent, enormous, its prisms throwing the flame back at her in pieces. She wiped a smear from the glass. She sat on the stool by the clock and listened to it.

That was the real duty, these nights. Not the lamp but the clock. Every two hours and forty minutes the weight reached the bottom of the tower and someone had to wind it back up — forty turns of the crank, her arms burning by the thirtieth — or the lens would slow, and stop, and the light would go from a flash to a stare, and a ship out there in the fog, counting, would see the wrong signal and think itself somewhere it was not.

Her father had always heard the clock. He used to say he could hear it in his sleep, that if it faltered he would wake before it stopped. She had seen him do it when she was a girl: sit up in the dark, already reaching for his boots, a full minute before the tick changed.

Three weeks ago she had woken at four for no reason and climbed up, and found the lens turning slow, the weight a hand's breadth from the floor, and her father on the stool with his eyes open, watching the flame, hearing nothing.

She had wound it without a word. Forty turns. He had looked at her afterwards as if she had struck him, and then he had gone down the stairs, and neither of them had spoken of it since. Some nights now she kept both halves.

---

At one in the morning the fog thinned for a few minutes, and she saw a light.

It was low and far out, west by north, a yellow point that came and went. Not a star; there were no stars. A ship, then — a boat fishing late, or the cargo boat from Horta running early, or someone lost. She took the binoculars from their hook and went out onto the gallery, into the cold, and the horn blew beneath her so that the iron rail shook in her hands.

The light was gone.

She stood there a long time, the beam going round over her head, white on white. Somewhere out in that, someone was counting. One-and-two-and. Two flashes every ten seconds. *Ponta Negra. You are here. You are not lost, not yet. Keep off.* It was the only sentence the tower knew, and it said it all night, every night, to people it would never see.

She thought: if I go, who says it?

And then, because she was nineteen and it was one in the morning, she was angry — at the thought, at the tower, at her father asleep below her, at her mother for dying and leaving the sentence to her. She went back inside and slammed the door of the lamp room hard enough to rattle the glass, and the lens went on turning as if nothing had happened, which was true; nothing had.

---

At ten past three the weight touched the floor.

She was ready for it. She set the crank in its socket and began to wind, and on the eleventh turn she heard the door at the bottom of the tower, and her father's step on the stairs.

He came up slowly. He stopped at the landing. He came on. When he reached the lamp room he stood in the doorway in his coat, with his boots unlaced, and watched her wind, and she did not stop and he did not offer.

When it was done — forty turns, her shoulders on fire — she straightened and looked at him.

"I woke," he said. "I don't know why. I just woke."

She wanted to believe him. She wanted him to have heard, through ninety-six steps and a stone floor and the horn and his own failing ears, the moment the weight touched down; she wanted the old miracle back.

"Go to bed, Pai," she said, very clearly, facing him. "I have it."

He looked past her at the lens, at the light going round in the white.

"The ship is due on Thursday," he said. "If the fog lifts."

"I know."

"Your aunt will write."

She said nothing. He had never spoken of her aunt in that voice before, the voice of someone pressing a bruise to see if it still hurt. She wondered how much he knew. She wondered what he had found in her drawer, or heard — no, not heard. Seen. He watched everything now. He had become a man made of eyes.

"Go to bed," she said again.

He went. She listened to him go down, to the stop at the landing, to the door. Then she sat on the stool beside the clock and put her hand flat on its iron housing, the way he put his hand on the wall of the horn house, and felt it tick into her palm, slow and heavy, the whole tower turning on it.

The horn blew. The fog held. Out in the dark, someone counted, and was not lost.
