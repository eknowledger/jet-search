# Chapter 2 — The supply ship

*Part One — Fog. Draft 2.*

---

The fog lifted on Wednesday night as if someone had pulled a sheet off a table, and on Thursday morning the *Santa Luzia* was there, sitting off the cove at Fajã das Garças with smoke going straight up from her funnel into a sky so clean it hurt to look at.

Elena saw her from the gallery at seven, while she was polishing the lens. She put down the cloth — chamois, never cotton; cotton scratched — and stood with her hands on the rail and looked. The ship was small and old and painted the colour of a dirty tooth, and she was the most beautiful thing Elena had ever seen, as she was every second Thursday.

Then the whistle came, two long notes across the water, the signal for the boats.

Below her, in the yard, her father was splitting wood for the stove. He did not look up. He brought the axe down and the log fell in two halves and he set another on the block. The whistle came again, and the gulls on the cliff rose all at once, screaming, and settled. Her father brought the axe down.

She went down the ninety-six steps faster than she should have.

"The ship," she said in the yard, in front of him, where he could see. "She's in."

He looked at the sea, and at her, and then at the axe in his hand as if he were surprised to find it there. "I saw her," he said. "Before you. At six."

Perhaps he had. He was up before her these days; she heard him moving below in the dark, making the coffee, sitting with it at the window. Perhaps he had seen the ship come round the point in the first grey light and said nothing, because he did not want to go down to the cove, where there would be men talking all at once and the boatmen shouting, and everyone's mouth moving and no way to watch them all.

"I'll go," she said. "You stay with the light."

"The paraffin," he said. "Twelve drums. Count them. Last time they sent ten and wrote twelve."

"I'll count them."

---

The path to the cove went down the cliff in long loops, like a ribbon let fall from a window. From the top she could see the whole village gathering — the women in black with baskets, the boys running ahead, old Zé from the whale lookout with his telescope under his arm as if he might need it — and on the black stones of the landing, the two village boats being run down into the water, the men waist-deep and shouting.

By the time she reached the bottom, the first boat was coming back from the ship, low in the water with sacks of flour and a crate of something that clinked, and, sitting on the crate in his white shirt with the mail bag across his knees like a child, Henrique.

He was not supposed to come ashore. He was the radio operator; his place was in the little cabin behind the bridge with his headphones and his Morse key and the voices of the whole Atlantic in his ears. But every second Thursday the captain let him bring the mail in, and every second Thursday he did, and the village women had stopped pretending not to know why.

He jumped out before the boat touched, into water to his knees, held the bag over his head, laughing, and waded up the stones to her.

"Menina Varga."

"Senhor Henrique. You're wet."

"I'm always wet. The sea is very wet this year." He was twenty-four, from Ponta Delgada, with a city boy's soft hands and a sunburned neck, and he talked faster than anyone she knew. "How is the keeper? How is the light? I saw it on Tuesday night — no, I didn't, I saw nothing, there was fog from Corvo to Newfoundland. We crawled. The captain swore in four languages. I listened to a Norwegian trawler talking to its mother for six hours." He was untying the bag. "You have letters."

"Letters?"

"Two. One official. One from Lisbon, in a lady's hand, smelling of —" he sniffed it, outrageously — "lavender and fried fish."

"Give it to me."

He gave it to her. She did not open it. She put both envelopes inside her coat, against her chest, and felt them there like a second heartbeat, and turned to the boats to count the drums.

---

There were twelve. She counted them three times as the men rolled them up the beach, and then she wrote the number in the little book her father had given her, and signed it, and made the boatman sign it too, while Henrique stood behind her reading over her shoulder and pretending not to.

"Twelve," he said. "He'll be happy."

"He'll be suspicious."

"Last time it was ten because the clerk in Horta is a thief. I told the captain. The captain told the clerk. The clerk told me he would have me sent to Angola." He said it lightly, the way everyone said *Angola* that year, as if the word were too hot to hold for long. "So. Twelve."

They went up the path together behind the mule, which carried two drums at a time and hated every step. Halfway up, at the bend where the path turned back on itself and you could see the whole western sea, Henrique stopped to breathe, or pretended to.

"I can hear Lisbon," he said.

"What?"

"On the set. At night, when the ship is quiet and the captain is asleep. The Emissora Nacional, and the ships in the Tagus, and the pilots, and once a woman on a yacht asking the port for a doctor. You turn the dial and the whole world is in there, talking. Fado at two in the morning." He looked at her sideways. "Your father's lighthouse has a radio."

"It's for the weather."

"It's for listening. Does he listen?"

She looked at the sea so as not to look at him. "He reads the bulletins. I copy them down."

Henrique was quiet for a moment, which was rare.

"On the fourteenth of November," he said, "we make the last crossing before winter. Flores, Faial, Terceira, São Miguel, and then the mainland ship from Ponta Delgada to Lisbon on the twentieth. The captain owes me a favour. Several favours. If someone wanted a berth —"

"Someone."

"If someone did."

The mule had gone on without them and was standing at the next bend, looking back with contempt.

"I have to take the drums up," Elena said.

---

She read the letter that night in the lamp room, by the light of the light.

*My dear girl,* Tia Lurdes wrote, in her round convent hand, *I have been to see the Sister at the nursing school, and she says there is a place in the January course if you are here by December, and she says nothing about money that I could not manage. I have cleared the little room at the back, the one that looks onto the steps. You will hear the tram going up to the castle all day and the neighbours all night, and you will hate it for a month and then you will love it, as I did. Come, filha. Your mother would have wanted it, whatever your father says. She wanted it for herself once.*

Elena read it four times. Then she read the last line again.

*She wanted it for herself once.*

She had not known that. No one had told her that. She sat with the letter on her knee and the lens turning above her and tried to imagine her mother — her mother who had smelled of bread and bleach, who had sung while she ironed and counted the stairs with her — wanting a city, a room over steps, a tram. She could not do it. And then, suddenly, she could, and that was worse.

The other envelope was from the Lighthouse Service in Lisbon. It was addressed to Tomás Varga, keeper. It said that an inspector would visit Ponta Negra in the course of October to carry out the periodic examination of the station and of its personnel, *including the medical fitness of the keeper*, and requested that all be in order.

She had opened it without thinking, because she opened all the post now, because he had asked her to — *your eyes are younger* — and now it lay open on the table next to her aunt's letter, the two of them side by side under the turning light, and she understood that she was going to have to choose which one to show him first.

Below her, in the dark, the door opened. She heard his step on the stairs, and the stop at the landing.

She folded her aunt's letter very small and put it inside her shoe.
