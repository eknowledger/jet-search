# Chapter 7 — Dona Graça

*Part One — Fog. Draft 1. The only chapter in first person: Graça narrates.*

---

I have brought four hundred and twelve children into this world, and I remember the weather for every one of them.

That is not boasting. It is what happens to you, in my trade, on an island like this. You remember the weather because the weather is what you were fighting. I remember the wind that took the roof off the Medeiros house while Conceição was pushing, and the snow — the only snow I ever saw on Flores, in 1931 — the night the Silveira twins came, and the August heat that sat on the village like a hen the year Manuel Freitas's boy was born blue and I breathed into him until he wasn't.

And I remember the fog the night Elena Varga came, in March of forty-three, because her mother wanted me to stop the horn.

"Make it stop," Amélia said. "Graça, for the love of God, make it stop." As if I could. As if anyone could stop that thing, which Tomás had started himself at dusk and which went on all night, two blasts and silence, two blasts and silence, until I felt it in my own womb, which had never carried anything. She was in the room under the lamp room — they had their bed there then, before — and every time it blew, the glass in the window shook and Amélia shook with it and cursed her husband and the lighthouse and the Republic and the sea. I had never heard her curse before. She was a gentle woman. Labour finds out what you are.

The child came at four, red and furious, mouth open, and Tomás was not there because he could not be; he was upstairs with the light. That is the first thing to understand about that family. He was always upstairs with the light.

---

I am telling this because Elena came to my house on Tuesday, and I have not slept properly since.

She came after dark, which a girl like her should not do, along the cliff path with no lamp. I heard her before I saw her. I have very good ears still; it is the only part of me that has not gone to ruin. I heard her stop at my gate and stand there, deciding, and I let her stand, because a person who is deciding should be allowed to finish.

Then she knocked, and I let her in and gave her the chair by the stove and a cup of the bad coffee, and I looked at her.

Nineteen. Her mother's mouth and her father's hands — big hands, the knuckles already red from the brass polish and the cold water. She had grown thin since the summer. She had the look I know from women in the last weeks before, when they have stopped being afraid of the pain and started being afraid of everything after it.

"Dona Graça," she said. "You were there when I was born."

"I was."

"Was my mother —" She stopped. She looked into the cup. "Did my mother ever want to leave? Flores. Did she ever want to go?"

So. Lurdes had written. I had always known Lurdes would write, one day; Lurdes had never in her life kept a thing in her mouth that could be spat out.

What do you say? I will tell you what I did not say. I did not say that Amélia sat in this kitchen, in that chair, in the winter of forty-one, a bride of a year, and cried because the ticket her sister had sent her from Lisbon was in her pocket and her husband did not know. I did not say that I told her to go. I did tell her to go. I was younger, and I thought a woman should take any door that opened. And I did not say that she came back the next week, after the ship had sailed without her, and laughed, and said she had used the ticket to light the stove.

What I said was: "Every girl on this island wants to leave, filha. Some of them do."

She looked at me then the way her father looks at people now — at the mouth, hard, as if she could read the words underneath the words. And I thought: she knows about him. Of course she knows. She lives in a tower with him.

---

Because I know about him too. I have known since Easter.

It was the bell. Padre Anselmo rings for the Elevation, and the whole church goes down on its knees, and Tomás Varga, who has knelt at that bell for nearly forty years, stayed standing. Only for a breath. Then he saw the people around him going down, and he went down too, quickly, like a man who has missed a step on the stairs. Nobody noticed. Nobody but me, and I notice everything; it is my trade. You learn to see the thing a body is trying to hide.

After that I watched him. He turns his whole head now when you speak, not just his eyes. He stands with his back to walls. He laughs half a second after everyone else. At the feast of the Holy Ghost in June, when the band played, he sat beside the big drum, and I saw him lay his hand on the skin of it between the songs, as if he were feeling for a fever.

A deaf keeper is not a keeper. I do not need Lisbon to tell me that. If the inspector finds him out, they will retire him on half pay to a house in Santa Cruz with no sea in the window, and he will die there in two winters, the way the old ones do when you take them from the thing they are for. I have seen it. I have laid them out.

And the girl knows all this. That is what she was really asking me, in my kitchen, with her mother's mouth. Not *did my mother want to go*. She was asking: *if I go, what happens to him?*

---

"Elena," I said. "Who winds the clock at night?"

She did not answer, which was the answer.

"Since when?"

"Since the spring." Very quiet. "The first watch. Sometimes both."

"And the inspector comes in October."

She put the cup down. "How do you know that?"

"Because the inspector comes every October, child, since before you were born, and stays with Padre Anselmo, and eats all his chouriço, and asks me whether there have been any fevers at the lighthouse." I took her hand. It was cold right through. "What do you want me to do?"

She told me. It did not take long. She wanted me to tell the inspector, if he asked, that Tomás Varga was as sound as a bell — she actually said that, *as sound as a bell*, and then heard herself, and we both, God forgive us, laughed. She wanted me to say I had examined him in the summer. She wanted, I understood, someone else to carry the lie for a while, so that she could set it down and look at it, and decide whether she could pick it up again for the rest of her life.

And then — she did not say this, but it was there in the room with us, as plain as the stove — when the lie was safe, she wanted to get on the November ship.

---

I walked her back to the top of the cliff path with my lamp. The light was going round above us, two flashes and the dark, two flashes and the dark. She stopped at the turning where you can see the tower and stood a long time looking at it.

"He used to hear the clock in his sleep," she said.

"I know."

"He used to wake up before it stopped."

"I know, filha."

She went down the path without the lamp. She said she knew every stone. I stood and watched the light until my legs hurt, thinking of Amélia in that chair, and the ticket in the stove, and what I had told her then, and what I would tell her daughter now, when the inspector sat down at Padre Anselmo's table and wiped his mouth and turned to me.

I have brought four hundred and twelve children into this world. I have never yet told a mother which way to push. You only tell them *now*, and *wait*, and *now* again.

I do not know yet which one I will say.
