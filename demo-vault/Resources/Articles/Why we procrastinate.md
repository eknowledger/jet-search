# Why we procrastinate

*My summary of a pile of reading on procrastination — mostly Tim Pychyl and Fuschia Sirois on emotions, Piers Steel on motivation, and Hal Hershfield on the future self. I wrote it in October 2026, after a week in which I reorganised the spice drawer instead of the novel, the landlord e-mail and the boiler booking. Nothing here is new; it's the version I need to remember.*

## It isn't a time-management problem

The single idea that changed how I think about it: procrastination is mostly about **mood**, not about time. We don't delay a task because we can't read a calendar. We delay it because the task makes us feel something unpleasant — boredom, anxiety, confusion, self-doubt, resentment — and putting it off makes that feeling go away *right now*. Tim Pychyl's line is that procrastination is "giving in to feel good". Fuschia Sirois calls it short-term mood repair at the expense of our future self.

That explains why it's so sticky. When I open the novel file, feel the dread of a blank scene and check the weather instead, I get a small hit of relief. The avoidance is rewarded, so it gets repeated. The task is still there, now with a layer of guilt on top, which makes it even more unpleasant next time. A loop.

It also explains why "just be more disciplined" doesn't help. Discipline is aimed at the clock. The problem sits in the feeling.

## What makes a task aversive

Pychyl lists seven features that make us more likely to put something off. A task gets delayed more when it is:

1. boring
2. frustrating
3. difficult
4. ambiguous
5. unstructured
6. lacking personal meaning
7. lacking intrinsic reward

Reading the list was slightly embarrassing, because it describes my own pile. The tax return: boring, ambiguous, no reward. Chapter 10 of [[The Lighthouse Keeper]]: difficult, unstructured and — on bad days — I can't see why it matters. The e-mail to the landlord about the [[Bedroom window leak]]: frustrating, because the last three went nowhere.

The useful part is that each feature suggests a counter-move. Ambiguous → write down the very next physical action. Unstructured → give it a shape (a time box, a checklist). Boring → pair it with something nice. Lacking meaning → remind myself why it's on the list at all, or admit it shouldn't be.

## The motivation equation

Piers Steel pulled decades of research together — a big 2007 review that pooled hundreds of studies — into what he calls temporal motivation theory. Roughly:

> Motivation = (Expectancy × Value) ÷ (Impulsiveness × Delay)

- **Expectancy** — how sure I am that I can do it
- **Value** — how rewarding it is, to me, not in theory
- **Impulsiveness** — how easily I'm pulled away by whatever is in front of me
- **Delay** — how far away the reward or the deadline is

It isn't physics, but it's a good diagnostic. When I'm stuck, one of the four is usually the culprit. The [[Half Marathon]] plan doesn't need much willpower because the race is a fixed date and every run gives an immediate feeling. The novel has an 80,000-word goal and the reward is years away: Delay is enormous. Hence the 600 words before breakfast — it turns one distant reward into a small daily one.

Steel's review found procrastination tied most strongly to impulsiveness and to how unpleasant the task is, and only weakly to perfectionism, which surprised me. Perfectionism shows up, but it's the *fear of being judged* part that matters, not the high standards.

## Present bias and the stranger in the future

Behavioural economists call the general pattern **present bias**: a reward today counts for much more than the same reward next week. And we're inconsistent about it — €100 today versus €110 tomorrow feels very different from €100 in a year versus €110 in a year and a day, even though the gap is the same.

Hal Hershfield's research adds a strange twist. In brain-imaging studies, thinking about yourself ten years from now looks more like thinking about another person than like thinking about yourself today. Future-me is, in the scanner, a bit of a stranger. So when I put off something unpleasant, I'm handing it to someone I don't feel much for. People who feel more connected to their future selves save more and procrastinate less; in one set of experiments, seeing an aged photo of themselves made people put more aside for retirement.

It's the same mechanism as in [[How compound interest works]]: the payoff is in the future, and the future doesn't feel like me.

## Self-forgiveness beats self-criticism

This is the bit I most needed. A study at Carleton University (Wohl, Pychyl and Bennett, 2010) followed first-year students through two exams. Students who **forgave themselves** for procrastinating before the first exam procrastinated *less* before the second. Beating yourself up doesn't fix it; it adds another bad feeling to the task, which makes avoiding it even more attractive.

Sirois's research on self-compassion points the same way. Self-compassion isn't letting yourself off the hook. It's treating the slip as normal and human ("everyone avoids things that feel bad"), noticing the feeling without drama, and then taking the next step anyway. Kindness lowers the emotional cost of going back.

When I lose a morning, the old script was "you'll never finish a novel". The new one, which I'm still practising, is "OK, that one got away. What's the smallest thing I can do now?"

## What actually helps — the list I keep

**1. Make the first step tiny and concrete.** Not "chapter 10" but "open the file and write one sentence of Elena on the lighthouse gallery". Starting is the expensive part. Once I'm in, the dread usually turns out to have been bigger than the task. "Just get started" is Pychyl's most repeated advice, and it sounds too simple until you try it.

**2. If–then plans.** Peter Gollwitzer's research on *implementation intentions* shows that plans in the form "if X, then I do Y" make people far more likely to follow through. Mine: *if it's 7:00 and the coffee is made, then I sit down with the novel open.* The decision is made in advance, so there's nothing to negotiate in the moment.

**3. Time boxes.** A fixed, short block gives an unstructured task a shape and makes the end visible. That's why [[The Pomodoro technique]] helps so much with admin: 25 minutes is a size my brain doesn't argue with.

**4. Temptation bundling.** Katy Milkman's study at the University of Pennsylvania gave people gripping audiobooks they could only listen to at the gym; gym visits went up. I've copied it: the good podcast is only for long runs, and the nice coffee from the bakery is only for admin mornings.

**5. Less friction for the task, more for the escape.** Phone in another room during the morning words. The novel file open by default. Make the avoidance one step harder and the task one step easier.

**6. Name the feeling.** Before I flee, I try to say what the task makes me feel: "this scene scares me because I don't know what Tomás would say." Naming it often shrinks it — and sometimes tells me what's actually missing. (In that case: I hadn't decided how deaf he is yet.)

**7. Be nice to future-me.** A line on the project page — "future Noa, you'll be glad the boiler is booked" — sounds silly. It helps a little.

**8. Forgive and restart.** See above. The next attempt is the only one that counts.

## What doesn't help

- Waiting to "feel like it". The mood comes *after* starting, not before.
- Big, vague resolutions on Sunday evening.
- Productive procrastination — doing a useful but easier task instead. (I am the world champion of reorganising the spice drawer.) It *feels* virtuous, which is exactly why it's dangerous.
- Shame. It's fuel for the loop.
