# The science of sourdough

*Summary of what I've read about what actually happens in a starter and a loaf — mostly from the Global Sourdough Project papers, a long piece in Serious Eats, and the sourdough chapters of Harold McGee's* On Food and Cooking. *For the practical side see [[Cooking]].*

## A starter is a tiny ecosystem

Flour and water left at room temperature get colonised by microbes that were already on the grain, in the flour, on our hands and in the kitchen air. After a week or two of feeding, the community settles down to two groups:

- **Wild yeasts** — often *Saccharomyces cerevisiae* (the same species as baker's yeast) or *Kazachstania humilis*. They make carbon dioxide (the rise) and a little alcohol.
- **Lactic acid bacteria** — the best known is *Fructilactobacillus sanfranciscensis*, named after San Francisco sourdough. They make lactic and acetic acid (the sour) and some CO₂ of their own.

The bacteria outnumber the yeasts by something like 100 to 1. They get along because they mostly want different sugars: the bacteria like maltose, which many of the sourdough yeasts can't use.

The acid is what keeps a starter stable. Most other microbes, including the ones that spoil food, can't cope with a pH around 3.5–4, so a healthy starter defends itself.

## Where does a starter's character come from?

The Global Sourdough Project (Rob Dunn's lab and collaborators, 2021) sequenced about 500 starters from around the world. The surprise: **geography barely predicted the microbes**. A starter in Lisbon and one in Seoul could be close cousins. What mattered more were things like how often it's fed, how old it is, the flour, and the temperature it lives at. The bakers' own hands carried some of the same bacteria as their starters, which is lovely and a bit gross.

## Lactic vs acetic: steering the sour

- **Lactic acid** tastes mild and creamy, like yoghurt.
- **Acetic acid** tastes sharp, like vinegar.

Rough rules of thumb:

| You want | Do |
|---|---|
| milder, more lactic | warmer (26–30 °C), wetter starter, use it at its peak |
| sharper, more acetic | cooler, stiffer starter, long cold retard in the fridge |

My starter Fermento is fed 1:5:5 at 26 °C — one part starter, five flour, five water — which keeps it on the mild side and peaks in about eight hours.

## What happens in the dough

1. **Mixing and autolyse.** Water lets enzymes in the flour start breaking starch into sugars, and two proteins (glutenin and gliadin) link up into gluten.
2. **Bulk fermentation.** Yeast inflate the gluten with CO₂; bacteria make acid. A little acid strengthens the gluten; a lot weakens it. That's why an over-fermented dough turns soupy.
3. **Shaping and proofing.** Building surface tension so the gas has somewhere to push.
4. **Baking.** Steam keeps the crust soft for the first minutes so the loaf can spring up. Then the surface dries and browns through the Maillard reaction and caramelisation — the bitter, nutty, toasty flavours.

## Things sourdough does that yeasted bread doesn't

- **Keeps longer.** The acids slow staling and mould.
- **Lower glycaemic response**, in several studies, than the same bread made with baker's yeast.
- **Minerals more available.** A long acidic fermentation breaks down phytic acid, which otherwise binds iron and zinc.
- **Some FODMAPs broken down** in long fermentations, which is why some people with IBS do better with it. It is **not** safe for coeliacs — the gluten is still there.

## Notes to self

- If Fermento smells of nail-polish remover, it's hungry. Feed it, don't panic.
- Dark liquid on top (hooch) = alcohol. Pour off or stir in for more sour.
- Cold slows everything; the fridge is a pause button, not a reset.
