# Sleep and memory

*Summary of a few review articles and a long podcast episode on how sleep helps us remember. Read because my [[Health]] numbers say I average 7 h 12 min a night (September) and I wanted to know whether that's enough for kanji.*

## The basic architecture

A night is a series of **cycles of roughly 90 minutes**. Each cycle goes through light sleep (N1, N2), deep or **slow-wave sleep** (N3), and **REM** sleep, where most vivid dreaming happens. Early in the night the cycles are heavy on deep sleep; towards morning they're heavy on REM. That's why cutting the night short costs mostly REM, and going to bed very late costs mostly deep sleep.

## What deep sleep does

The leading idea is called *active systems consolidation*:

- During the day the **hippocampus** quickly stores new facts and events — a fast but small inbox.
- During slow-wave sleep, it **replays** those memories. Recordings in rats show the same sequence of neurons firing during sleep as when the rat ran the maze, just faster.
- Three rhythms line up — slow oscillations from the cortex, **sleep spindles** (short bursts in N2 and N3), and tiny "ripples" in the hippocampus. When they coincide, the memory seems to be passed to the cortex for long-term storage.

The result: facts and word lists (declarative memory) are better after a night's sleep than after the same hours awake.

## What REM does

Less settled. REM seems to matter for **emotional memories** — keeping the content while taking some of the sting out — and possibly for finding links between ideas. Motor skills (a piano piece, a typing sequence) improve overnight too, linked with spindles in N2 sleep late in the night.

## Classic studies worth remembering

- **Jenkins and Dallenbach, 1924**: people remembered nonsense syllables better after sleeping than after the same time awake. The first hint that sleep protects memory, not just rests the body.
- **Rasch et al., 2007**: people learned card locations while smelling roses. Smelling roses again during *deep sleep* improved recall the next day. Not during REM, not while awake. Smells can cue the replay.
- **Sleep before learning matters too**: after a night without sleep, people's ability to store new memories drops by something like 40%. The inbox doesn't empty.
- **Mazza et al., 2016**: people who learned word pairs in the evening, slept, and relearned in the morning needed fewer tries and remembered more six months later than people who did the same in one day.

## Caveats

- A lot of popular writing on sleep overstates the effects; some numbers in bestsellers turned out shakier than they sounded.
- Most studies are small and in young adults.
- "Sleep more" is good advice, but stressing about sleep is bad for sleep.

## What I'm doing with it

- **Kanji and Spanish reviews in the evening**, a quick re-test in the morning — the Mazza pattern.
- **No screens in bed** and the same wake-up time on weekends, as far as [[Rui]] and Pimenta allow.
- **Morning words for [[The Lighthouse Keeper]]** after a full night, not after a short one — the bad-sleep pages are always the ones I delete.
- A 20-minute nap is fine; longer and I wake up groggy (I land in deep sleep).
- Track it in [[Health]] for a month and see if the 7 h 12 min moves.
