# How compound interest works

*My summary, pieced together from the Banco de Portugal's "Todos Contam" pages, a Khan Academy video and an argument with Rui over dinner. Written so I stop having to look it up.*

## The idea

Simple interest pays you on what you put in. Compound interest pays you on what you put in **plus** the interest you've already earned. Interest earns interest. Over a year or two the difference is tiny. Over twenty or thirty years it's most of the money.

## The formula

> A = P × (1 + r/n)^(n × t)

- **P** — what you start with
- **r** — the yearly rate, as a decimal (5% = 0.05)
- **n** — how many times a year interest is added (1 = yearly, 12 = monthly)
- **t** — years

## €1,000 at 5%

| Years | Simple interest | Compound (yearly) |
|---|---:|---:|
| 10 | €1,500 | €1,629 |
| 20 | €2,000 | €2,653 |
| 30 | €2,500 | €4,322 |

Compounding monthly instead of yearly, 30 years gives €4,468; continuously, €4,482. So how *often* it compounds matters a little. The rate and, above all, the time matter a lot.

## The rule of 72

Divide 72 by the rate to get roughly how many years it takes to double.

- 5% → 14.4 years (the exact answer is 14.2)
- 2% → 36 years
- 9% → 8 years

It applies to inflation too: at 3% a year, prices double in about 24 years.

## Saving a bit every month

€150 a month at 5%, compounded monthly:

| For | Paid in | Ends up as |
|---|---:|---:|
| 10 years | €18,000 | €23,292 |
| 20 years | €36,000 | €61,655 |

Twice the years, more than two and a half times the money. That's the whole argument for starting early, in one table.

## The catches

- **Inflation.** The real return is roughly the rate minus inflation. 2% interest with 2% inflation is standing still.
- **Tax.** In Portugal 28% is withheld on interest from savings accounts, so 2.1% becomes about 1.5%.
- **Fees compound too.** €1,000 at 5% for 30 years is €4,322; at 4% (a 1% yearly fee) it's €3,243. A quarter gone to the fee.
- **Debt compounds the same way.** A credit card at 18% doubles what you owe in about four years.

## What I took from it

- The [[Emergency fund]] stays in a savings account. It's for access, not growth, and barely beating inflation is fine.
- The long-term money — the €188 a month into the index fund — is where time does the heavy lifting (see [[Savings rate]]).
- Starting early beats saving more later. Annoying but true.
- Future money feels abstract, which is the same trap as in [[Why we procrastinate]]: the reward is far away and doesn't feel like mine.
