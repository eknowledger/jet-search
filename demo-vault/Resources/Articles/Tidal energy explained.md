# Tidal energy explained

*Notes from a long explainer I read after watching the Tagus come in and out under the 25 de Abril bridge for an hour. Plus bits from the IEA and a BBC piece on the Scottish turbines.*

## Where tides come from

The Moon's gravity pulls on the oceans a bit more on the side of the Earth facing it, and a bit less on the far side, so the water bulges on both sides. As the Earth turns through those two bulges, most coasts get **two high and two low tides a day**, every 12 hours 25 minutes or so — which is why high tide is about 50 minutes later each day.

The Sun does the same thing at a bit under half the strength. When Sun and Moon line up (new and full moon) the effects add up: **spring tides**, the biggest range. When they're at right angles: **neap tides**, the smallest. The cycle takes about two weeks.

The shape of the coast does the rest. The Bay of Fundy in Canada funnels the water into a range of up to 16 m. Lisbon gets up to about 4 m on spring tides.

## Two ways to get power out of it

**1. Barrages and lagoons — using the height.**
A dam across an estuary lets the tide fill the basin, then releases the water through turbines (or generates both ways). It's hydropower with the Moon as the pump.

- **La Rance**, Brittany, France — opened in 1966, 240 MW. Still running.
- **Sihwa Lake**, South Korea — 254 MW, opened 2011, now the largest.

**2. Tidal stream — using the current.**
Underwater turbines, like wind turbines on the seabed, in places where the tide rushes through a narrow channel.

- **MeyGen**, in the Pentland Firth off the north of Scotland — four 1.5 MW turbines running since 2017–2018, with plans for much more.

Water is about 800 times denser than air, so a current of 2–3 m/s carries a lot of energy through a small rotor. The power goes up with the **cube** of the speed: double the current, eight times the power. That's why the sites are so specific — a little more speed changes everything.

## Why it's interesting

- **Predictable.** Tide tables are known years in advance. Wind and sun aren't. Grid operators love that.
- **Dense.** Small machines for the output.
- **Long-lived.** La Rance is nearly 60 years old.

## Why there isn't more of it

- **Few good sites.** You need a big range or a fast channel, near a grid and a port.
- **Expensive.** Building in salt water with storms is hard. Corrosion, things growing on everything, maintenance by boat.
- **Not constant.** Predictable isn't the same as steady: output drops to zero around each turn of the tide, four times a day.
- **Barrages change estuaries.** Sediment, fish, the mudflats birds feed on. The Swansea Bay tidal lagoon in Wales was turned down in 2018, mainly on cost.

## Tidal vs wave

Easy to confuse. **Wave** energy comes from the wind blowing across the sea; **tidal** comes from gravity. Portugal has tried wave power more than tidal: the Aguçadoura wave farm off Póvoa de Varzim in 2008 was one of the first in the world, and there have been tests at Peniche.

## My takeaways

- A small but reliable piece of the clean-energy puzzle, not the whole thing.
- The cube rule is the most useful fact here — it explains both why it's so attractive and why it only pays off in a few places.
- Next time the Tagus is racing out past Belém, that is a few hundred megawatts nobody is using.
