# A short history of the Lisbon earthquake of 1755

*Summary in my words after reading Nicholas Shrady's* The Last Day *and a few museum texts — the Lisboa Story Centre on Praça do Comércio and the Carmo ruins. Written because I live in the neighbourhood that came through it.*

## The morning

**1 November 1755**, All Saints' Day, around 9:40 in the morning. Much of the city was at mass, churches full, candles lit everywhere for the feast.

Three shocks came in the space of about ten minutes. Modern estimates put the earthquake at around **magnitude 8.5–9**, with the epicentre in the Atlantic some 200 km south-west of Cape St. Vincent. Survivors wrote about cracks metres wide opening in the streets of the centre. Churches collapsed onto their congregations.

## Then the water, then the fire

People ran for the open space down by the river, on the old palace square and the new marble quay. About **forty minutes** after the quake the sea pulled back, showing the riverbed and old wrecks — and then the **tsunami** came up the Tagus. Waves of several metres swept the waterfront; in the Algarve and in Morocco they were higher still.

Then the fires. The candles from All Saints' and the kitchen hearths set the ruins alight, and the city burned for **five or six days**.

## The losses

- Deaths in Lisbon: usually estimated at **30,000–50,000**, out of about 200,000 people. The numbers are still argued about.
- The **Ribeira Palace** by the river, with the royal library of some 70,000 books, maps and archives from the age of the voyages.
- The new **opera house**, the Ópera do Tejo, opened only months before.
- The great **All Saints Royal Hospital** at Rossio.
- The **Carmo Convent**, whose roofless Gothic arches are still standing on purpose — today they hold the archaeology museum.

**Alfama** came through much better than the low-lying centre, probably because it sits on harder rock. A lot of its tangled medieval street plan is still there, which is why it looks the way it does outside our windows.

## Rebuilding

King José I — who, it's said, never slept under a stone roof again and lived in a wooden palace at Ajuda — put his minister **Sebastião José de Carvalho e Melo**, later the Marquis of Pombal, in charge of the recovery. The famous order "bury the dead and care for the living" is attributed to him (or to someone in his circle).

What came next was remarkably modern:

- The **Baixa** was rebuilt as a grid of straight, wide streets, designed by the military engineers Manuel da Maia, Eugénio dos Santos and Carlos Mardel.
- Buildings were standardised and built around a **gaiola pombalina** — a flexible timber cage inside the masonry, meant to sway instead of fall. Models were reportedly tested by having soldiers march around them.
- The palace square became **Praça do Comércio**, open to the river.

## The first seismology?

Pombal sent a questionnaire to every parish: how long did the shaking last, which direction, how many aftershocks, did the sea rise, did the wells change? The answers, still in the archives, are one reason the earthquake is often called the start of modern seismology.

## The argument in Europe

The disaster shook more than buildings. Why would God destroy a pious city on a holy day?

- **Voltaire** wrote the *Poem on the Lisbon Disaster* (1756) and, later, *Candide* (1759), mocking the idea that this is the best of all possible worlds.
- **Rousseau** answered that nature hadn't built six-storey houses packed together — people had.
- **Kant**, young then, wrote three short texts on the causes of earthquakes, one of the first attempts at a natural explanation.

## Walking it

- Start at the Carmo ruins, down to Rossio, through the Baixa grid to Praça do Comércio.
- Look for the Pombaline buildings: same height, same windows, stone ground floors.
- End in Alfama, where the streets stop being straight. Home.
