# Composition — rule of thirds and beyond

Composition is deciding what's in the frame, where, and what's left out. The rule of thirds is the first tool everyone learns. It's a good start and a bad finish.

## The rule of thirds

Divide the frame into a 3 × 3 grid. Put the subject on one of the lines, or on one of the four points where they cross, instead of in the centre. Put the horizon on the top or bottom line, not across the middle.

- It helps because it creates a little tension and leaves space for the subject to "look into".
- On the X-T5, turn on the 9-square framing guideline in the screen settings.
- A person walking: leave the space in front of them, not behind.

## Beyond the thirds

### Leading lines
Lines that pull the eye into the picture: tram rails, staircases, the edge of a wall, a row of street lamps. Alfama is made of them. Stand at the bottom of a staircase and let the steps carry the eye up to a figure at the top.

### Frames within the frame
Doorways, arches, windows, a gap between two buildings. A frame focuses attention and adds depth. The arches of the Sé, or a laundry line framing the river.

### Layers
Foreground, middle ground, background, each with something in it. Hard, and the most rewarding when it comes off. Alex Webb's colour street photographs are the masterclass. Try: a shoulder in the foreground, a tram in the middle, a hill with houses behind.

### Negative space
Let a small subject sit in a lot of empty space: one figure against a white wall, a single gull over the Tejo. Emptiness makes the subject feel important, or lonely.

### Symmetry and the centre
Sometimes the centre is right: a symmetrical façade, a long corridor, a reflection. The arch of Rua Augusta wants to be dead centre. If you break the thirds, break them on purpose.

### Light first
The best composition in dull light loses to an average one in great light. Look for the light first, then build the frame around it ([[Golden hour and blue hour]]).

### Juxtaposition
Two things in the frame that talk to each other: an old woman beside a poster of a model, a cat sitting under a "no animals" sign. Hard to plan; easy to miss.

### Figure and ground
A dark subject against a light background, or the reverse. The simplest way to make a subject stand out. Squint at the scene: if the subject disappears, the picture will too.

## Waiting for the moment

Henri Cartier-Bresson called it the decisive moment. In practice: find a good frame (light, lines, background) and wait for someone to walk into it. Most of my good street photos took ten minutes of standing still for one second of luck.

## Exercises

- [ ] One walk where every photo uses a leading line.
- [ ] One walk with nothing but frames within frames.
- [ ] Ten photos with the subject dead centre, on purpose.
- [ ] Pick one good spot and wait 20 minutes. Count what walks through.

## Checklist before pressing

1. What's the subject?
2. What's distracting at the edges?
3. Is the horizon straight?
4. Would one step left, right, closer or lower make it better? (Usually: closer.)
